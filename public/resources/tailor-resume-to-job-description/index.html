A practical, evidence-first method

How to tailor a resume to a job description

Turn a long job posting into a focused resume by changing the emphasis—not the truth.

On this page
  1. Read the role in three passes
  2. Build a requirement-to-evidence map
  3. Edit in order of impact
  4. Before and after: clearer evidence, same experience
  5. A ten-minute final check

Short answer

Start with evidence, not a score.

Read the job description for priorities, not just repeated words. Separate essential requirements from preferences, find the strongest real evidence for each priority, then move, trim or clarify that evidence in a separate copy of your resume. Keep your master resume unchanged.

1. Read the role in three passes

Trying to edit while you read usually produces scattered changes. First understand the role.

  1. Purpose: What outcome is this person hired to create?
  2. Requirements: Which qualifications appear mandatory, preferred or unclear?
  3. Language: Which specific tools, methods, customers or domains does the employer name?

A phrase appearing several times can signal importance, but repetition alone is not proof that it belongs in your resume. It still needs to describe something you have actually done or learned. The free job requirement extractor can create a traceable first pass while keeping ambiguous wording ambiguous.

2. Build a requirement-to-evidence map

For each central requirement, point to evidence in your work, education, projects, research, volunteering or caregiving. Use four honest labels:

DemonstratedYour resume already shows clear evidence.
Present but hiddenYou have evidence, but the current wording obscures it.
AdjacentYou have comparable experience, not the exact item.
GapYou cannot support the requirement yet.

A gap is useful information. It may affect whether you apply, what you learn next or what you explain in a cover letter. It is not an instruction to insert the missing phrase.

3. Edit in order of impact

Start where readers get the most context:

  • Clarify the target role in your headline or summary only if it accurately fits your background.
  • Move the most relevant roles, projects or bullets into easier view.
  • Rewrite vague bullets so they show the work, scope and result.
  • Name a tool or method when you genuinely used it and the detail helps.
  • Trim material that competes with stronger evidence for this role.

Dates, employers, credentials and outcomes do not change just because the posting uses different language.

Before and after: clearer evidence, same experience

Job priorityCoordinate customer onboarding across sales and support.

BeforeHelped new customers and worked with other teams.

AfterCoordinated onboarding for new business accounts with sales and support, maintained handoff notes, and resolved setup questions.

The revision does not add a team size, result or software the applicant cannot prove. It makes the existing coordination work visible using accurate language from the role.

A ten-minute final check

  • Can every claim be explained with a real example?
  • Are the role’s most important requirements easy to find?
  • Did you preserve required dates, qualifications and context?
  • Does the resume still sound like you?
  • Is this saved as a separate job-specific version?
  • Would the document make sense without the job posting beside it?
What not to do

Do not paste a list of “missing keywords,” hide text, rename yourself to an inaccurate title or turn exposure into expertise. A closer word match is not worth a weaker interview conversation.
