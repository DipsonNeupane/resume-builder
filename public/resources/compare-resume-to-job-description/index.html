How to compare your resume to a job description

The useful question is not “What is my match score?” It is “What can my resume actually demonstrate?”

On this page
  1. Turn the posting into a short requirement list
  2. Make a requirement-to-evidence table
  3. Interpret the gaps instead of counting them
  4. Choose one action for each item
  5. What a good comparison leaves you with

Short answer

Break the posting into individual requirements, label each as required, preferred or contextual, and connect it to a specific line in your resume. Mark evidence as clear, partial, absent or genuinely incompatible. Then decide whether to clarify the resume, find better evidence, accept a gap or skip the role.

Turn the posting into a short requirement list

Job descriptions mix outcomes, tasks, qualifications and company language. Reduce the posting to items you can evaluate:

  • Eligibility and hard constraints: licence, work authorization, location, schedule or required credential.
  • Core work: the outcomes and responsibilities the person will own.
  • Tools and domain knowledge: software, methods, customers, regulations or subject matter.
  • Preferences: useful advantages that are not stated as minimums.

Combine duplicate ideas. “Partner with sales,” “work cross-functionally” and “manage stakeholder handoffs” may describe one central collaboration requirement rather than three separate boxes. If the posting is long, use the free requirement extractor and verify every result against its source text.

Make a requirement-to-evidence table

RequirementResume evidenceStatus
Run monthly reportingPrepared weekly service reports and presented trendsPossibly relevant—check this
Advanced ExcelBuilt pivot-table reporting workbookShown in your resume
Professional licenceUser confirmed the licence is not heldYou said you don’t meet this requirement

“Possibly relevant—check this” is not automatically weak. Weekly reporting may be credible evidence for monthly reporting. But it should not be relabelled “five years of monthly reporting” if that did not happen.

Interpret the gaps instead of counting them

Ten minor preferences do not necessarily outweigh one central requirement. One missing legal credential may matter more than ten demonstrated soft skills. Rank the gaps by consequence:

  1. Does it affect eligibility or safe/legal performance?
  2. Is it central to the day-to-day work?
  3. Could adjacent experience transfer?
  4. Could it reasonably be learned after starting?
  5. Does the posting call it required, preferred or simply mention it?

This produces a decision you can explain. A percentage collapses those different questions into one number.

Choose one action for each item

  • Keep: the evidence is already clear.
  • Clarify: accurate experience is present but vague.
  • Add: relevant real evidence exists elsewhere in your master resume.
  • Explain: adjacent experience needs context in a cover letter or application answer.
  • Accept the gap: apply without pretending to have it.
  • Stop: a hard constraint makes the role unsuitable.

What a good comparison leaves you with

  • A short, prioritized requirement list
  • A real resume line beside every supported requirement
  • Uncertain items kept uncertain
  • Gaps visible enough to inform your decision
  • A small set of justified edits—not a wall of suggested keywords
